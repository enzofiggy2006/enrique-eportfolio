01 — About

Show yourself

Who I am going into this course, and what I was trying to get better at.

“Communication is a core business skill — it shapes how professionals present themselves, collaborate, and earn trust.”

I am Enrique Pelayo Figueroa, a business student at De La Salle University who is continuously developing my communication, teamwork, and professional skills. Throughout COMSKIL, I learned that effective communication goes beyond simply speaking or writing — it also involves understanding an audience, choosing the right message and medium, and presenting ideas with clarity and credibility.

This course helped me become more confident in expressing my ideas and more aware of how communication can influence business relationships and decisions. I hope to carry these skills into future academic projects, professional opportunities, and collaborative business environments.

02 — Lesson Highlights

What the term covered

A week-by-week summary of the concepts and how they translate to real business settings.

Week 1

Introduction to the Course

COMSKIL opened by setting the purpose, requirements, and expectations of the course. The biggest takeaway: communication is a core business skill that shapes how professionals present themselves, collaborate, and work with stakeholders — and understanding what's expected of you early makes you effective faster.

Weeks 1–2

Business Communication Foundation: the 8 C's, Grice's Theory, and Email Writing

I learned the 8 C's of effective communication and Grice's Theory of Pragmatics — being clear, concise, complete, correct, concrete, coherent, considerate, and courteous, while fitting the message to the situation. Applied to email and digital messages, these principles prevent misunderstandings, save time, and build a professional impression.

Weeks 3–4

Persuasive Messages, Nonverbal Language, and Paralinguistic Cues

Persuasion isn't only in the words. Gestures, posture, facial expressions, eye contact, tone, pitch, and pace all shape how a message lands. In business, these cues matter most when pitching an idea, negotiating, presenting a product, or building trust with a client.

Weeks 5–6

Critical Thinking: Non-Prose Forms (NPF) and Data Commentaries (DC)

I learned to turn business data into charts, tables, and graphs, then explain what the data actually means through a structured data commentary — identifying trends, making comparisons, and giving evidence-based recommendations. This is exactly how entrepreneurs and managers communicate market information to support decisions.

Weeks 7–9

Employment Communication: Resume, Cover Letter, and Job Interview

This block covered how to communicate qualifications professionally through a resume and cover letter, and how to prepare for an interview — alongside business politeness, the Politeness Principle, and the Aristotelian appeals of ethos, pathos, and logos. A candidate has to be clear about qualifications while also showing credibility and awareness of what the employer needs.

Week 8

Independent Learning Week

No class or new assignment this week — instead, time to review earlier lessons and manage my own pace. It's a small but real preview of professional life: entrepreneurs and employees are often responsible for their own learning and deadlines without someone checking in.

Weeks 10–11

The Three V's and Business Communication for Success

I learned about the Three V's — Visual, Verbal, and Vocal — and how they work together to make communication effective. The course also brought in Simon Sinek on understanding people and business, John Mullins' counter-conventional mindsets for business success, and Guy Kawasaki on innovation. Strong business communication needs a good message and an understanding of people, innovation, and value.

Week 12

Communicate Professionalism and Credibility

This lesson centered on career growth and building professionalism and credibility as you develop in the workplace. Growth means continuously improving how you communicate, behave, and present yourself — because credibility is often the deciding factor in whether clients and employers trust your ideas.

Week 13

Business Etiquette, Ethics, Teamwork, and Meetings

Effective workplace communication needs to be backed by proper etiquette, ethical behavior, teamwork, and productive meetings — both soft skills and hard skills, plus ethics and hiring considerations. Organizations run on people who can collaborate respectfully, act responsibly, and communicate professionally.

Week 14

Final Integrating Project: Individual E-Portfolio

The final project brings every communication skill from the term together into one deliverable — presenting my learning journey, work, and reflections in a portfolio I can actually use. It's the most direct rehearsal for real professional life: communicating your experience clearly to an employer, partner, or collaborator who's never met you.

Week 1 · Participation

Self-Introduction

My first self-introduction activity for the course, meant to demonstrate professional and personal communication from day one.

Reflection

Watching this back later in the term was humbling — I relied heavily on filler words and rushed through my achievements instead of framing them. My groupmates in the following weeks pointed out that I tend to undersell myself when speaking live, even when my writing is more confident. That single piece of feedback became something I actively worked against for the rest of the term, and it's the clearest before-and-after marker in this whole portfolio.

Week 2 · A1

Sell Yourself — Personal Pitch Video

A 1–2 minute video pitch presenting my personal brand, strengths, experience, and value, as if speaking to a potential client, investor, or collaborator.

Reflection

This was the first time I had to compress everything about myself into 90 seconds without losing what made it convincing. I leaned on the 8 C's we'd just learned — especially being concrete and concise — to cut vague claims like ‘I'm a hard worker’ down to specific, provable examples. My professor's feedback was that my pacing sped up whenever I got to the parts I was least confident about, which was a useful, slightly uncomfortable thing to notice about myself.

Week 2 · A2

Communication Analysis Reflection

A 200–400 word reflection analyzing a real communication experience using the 8 C's of Effective Communication and Grice's Maxims.

Reflection

Breaking down a real conversation using the 8 C's and Grice's Maxims made me realize how often miscommunication comes from violating a maxim without meaning to — usually being less clear or less relevant than the listener expected. Writing the analysis sharpened how I edit my own messages now: before sending anything important, I actually check it against these principles instead of just proofreading for grammar.

Refine Your Nonverbal and Verbal Cues

A reflection on my self-introduction video identifying strengths, areas for improvement, and action-oriented promises using the Visual, Verbal, and Vocal dimensions of communication.

Reflection

Going back to review my own delivery through the Visual-Verbal-Vocal framework was more clarifying than any single piece of outside feedback. I noticed I hold eye contact well but that my hands stay stiff and low, which reads as less confident than I actually feel. I turned that observation into a concrete promise — open gestures at chest height — that I tried to apply in every recorded task afterward.

Group NPF and DC Presentation

Reflection

Presenting as a group meant reconciling four different interpretations of the same data into one coherent narrative, which took more negotiation than I expected. I ended up taking the lead on the recommendations section since that was where our disagreements were sharpest, and had to practice stating a position clearly without shutting down groupmates who saw it differently. It was a good early test of the persuasive and nonverbal lessons from Weeks 3–4.

Mock Cover Letter and Resume

A tailored mock resume and cover letter for a target job or internship, applying persuasion appeals, the 8 C's, and Grice's Maxims.

Reflection

Tailoring the letter to one specific role forced me to actually research the company instead of writing something reusable. Applying ethos, pathos, and logos deliberately — rather than by instinct — showed me that my drafts usually lean too hard on logos (qualifications) and barely touch pathos (why I actually want the role), which is something I'm still correcting.
